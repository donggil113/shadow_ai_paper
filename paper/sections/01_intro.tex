\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A credit model is trained on loans that were granted, a diagnostic model on
patients who were tested, a recidivism model on defendants who were released, a
moderation model on posts that were reviewed, a hiring model on candidates who
were interviewed. In each case a decision maker chose who would be labelled,
using information $S$ --- demeanour, presentation, the case file --- absent
from the training set. Because $S$ drives both the decision $T$ and the outcome
$Y$, the labelled units are not a random sample of the deployment population,
and every reported test-set metric describes a population the model will never
face. This is the \emph{selective labels} problem
\citep{lakkaraju2017selective, kleinberg2018human}. Deployment risk is then not
merely hard to estimate but \emph{not identified}: the labels are missing for
reasons the data cannot see.

Current practice assumes the problem away. Empirical risk minimisation on
labelled units optimises the wrong risk. Inverse-propensity and doubly-robust
weighting \citep{robins1994estimation, chernozhukov2018double} correct for
selection on \emph{observables}, i.e.\ assume $S$ does not exist. Parametric
selection models \citep{heckman1979sample} admit unobservables but buy identification with an untestable joint-normality assumption indexed by
no interpretable notion of strength. Manski bounds
\citep{manski1990nonparametric} are assumption-free and, as we confirm, too wide
to support any decision.

\paragraph{This paper.} We take the middle road of causal sensitivity analysis
and build the learning theory around it. Our model $\dcsm(\Gamma)$ says the
censored units' outcome log-odds differ from the labelled units' by at most
$\log\Gamma$ at every covariate value; $\Gamma=1$ is selection on observables
and $\Gamma=\infty$ is Manski. In between, the identified set is an axis-aligned
box and everything becomes computable. Figure~\ref{fig:compas} is the representative result: on COMPAS, real release
decisions whose censored labels were nevertheless recorded, so the bounds can be
checked against the truth. There, coverage is $0\%$ under
missing-at-random, and $100\%$ exactly at the $\Gamma$ implied by the data's own
detained-versus-released odds ratio. Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sharp bounds for a ranking metric} (\cref{sec:ranking}).
  Fixed-threshold metrics are linear-fractional and easy to bound; AUROC is
  neither convex nor concave over the identified set.
  \Cref{thm:rank-identity} gives the exact identity, for mid-ranks $R$ and
  $\pi = \E[p]$,
  \[
    \AUC(p) = \frac{\langle p, R\rangle - \pi^2/2}{\pi(1-\pi)},
  \]
  so \emph{given the prevalence, AUROC is linear}; \cref{thm:sharp-auc} turns
  this into an exact $O(n\log n)$ algorithm for the sharp interval,
  \cref{prop:unified} extends it to a class containing every fixed-threshold
  metric, and evaluating at the corners of the identified set is \emph{not
  valid} because it ignores the coupling through the prevalence.

\item \textbf{A learning algorithm, not an audit} (\cref{sec:learning}).
  \Cref{thm:dcl} minimises worst-case risk over the identified set: the inner
  supremum is the risk at the midpoint model plus a margin penalty weighted by
  the local identification width, so the exact objective is convex with
  \emph{no relaxation} (unlike divergence-ball DRO), and the Bayes act is an
  \emph{interval-shrunk logit} with an abstention band. \Cref{prop:regret}
  gives the companion minimax-\emph{regret} rule in closed form as a difference
  quotient of the binary entropy.

\item \textbf{$\Gamma$ is unavoidable, but falsifiable}
  (\cref{sec:impossibility}). \Cref{thm:impossible} is a finite-sample
  two-point argument: for \emph{every} $n$, no estimator of deployment risk is
  accurate uniformly over censoring mechanisms, and $\Gamma$ is not identified
  from $(X, T, TY)$. \Cref{prop:falsify} is the converse: when
  decision makers of differing leniency handle comparable cases, a \emph{lower}
  bound $\Gamma_{\min}$ is identified; across 30 benchmark configurations it is
  valid without exception, never refutes a missing-at-random mechanism, and
  recovers $34$--$61\%$ of $\log\Gamma_0$.

\item \textbf{Generalisation, linear in $\log\Gamma$}
  (\cref{sec:generalization}). \Cref{thm:generalization} bounds the excess
  worst-case risk of the empirical minimiser with a complexity term growing like
  $\log\Gamma$, not $\Gamma$, because $\Gamma$ is a \emph{translation} on the
  log-odds scale. The empirical scaling matches.

\item \textbf{Uncertainty quantification is biased, undetectably}
  (\cref{sec:uncertainty}). \Cref{cor:uq} shows the observed-data regression
  $p_1(x)$ always lies \emph{inside} the identified set, so no calibration or
  goodness-of-fit check can refute the naive aleatoric/epistemic split. The
  epistemic term vanishes with $n$ while the identification width does not, so
  every two-way decomposition is asymptotically overconfident.
\end{itemize}
