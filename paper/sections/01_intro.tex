\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A credit model is trained on loans that were granted. A diagnostic model is
trained on patients who were tested. A recidivism model is trained on defendants
who were released. In each case an incumbent decision maker --- a loan officer, a
clinician, a judge --- chose who would be labelled, and chose using information
that never entered the training set: the applicant's demeanour, the patient's
presentation, the case file. Call that private information $S$. Because $S$
drives both the decision $T$ and the outcome $Y$, the labelled sub-population is
not a random sample of the deployment population, and the quantity every
practitioner reports --- test-set accuracy, AUROC, calibration --- measures
performance on a population the model will never face.

This is the \emph{selective labels} problem \citep{lakkaraju2017selective,
kleinberg2018human}. It is not a corner case: it is the default structure of
prediction in medicine, credit, criminal justice, content moderation, hiring and
fraud. Its consequence is stark. The deployment risk of a model is not merely
hard to estimate; under selective labels it is \emph{not identified}. No amount
of data fixes it, because the missing labels are missing for reasons the data
cannot see.

The field's usual responses each assume the problem away. Training on the
labelled units (empirical risk minimisation on the selected sample) silently
optimises the wrong risk. Inverse-propensity and doubly-robust weighting
\citep{robins1994estimation, chernozhukov2018double} correct for selection on
\emph{observables} and are exactly valid when $S$ does not exist. Parametric
selection models \citep{heckman1979sample} do allow selection on unobservables,
but buy identification with a joint-normality assumption that is untestable and
not indexed by any interpretable notion of strength. Assumption-free Manski
bounds \citep{manski1990nonparametric} are valid but, as we confirm, typically
too wide to support any decision.

\paragraph{This paper.} We take the middle road that has proved productive in
causal inference --- a \emph{sensitivity model} --- and develop the machine
learning that goes with it. Our sensitivity model, $\dcsm(\Gamma)$, says that the
censored units' outcome log-odds differ from the labelled units' by at most
$\log\Gamma$, at every covariate value. $\Gamma=1$ is selection on observables;
$\Gamma=\infty$ is Manski. In between, the identified set for the outcome
regression is an axis-aligned box, and everything becomes computable.

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sharp bounds for a ranking metric} (\cref{sec:ranking}).
  Existing sensitivity analysis bounds metrics defined at a \emph{fixed
  threshold} --- sensitivity, specificity, PPV --- which are linear-fractional in
  the outcome regression and therefore easy. AUROC is not: it is a ratio of a
  bilinear form to a product of linear forms, and is neither convex nor concave
  over the identified set. \Cref{thm:rank-identity} resolves this with an exact
  identity: writing $R(x)$ for the mid-rank of $x$ under the score,
  $\AUC(p) = (\langle p, R\rangle - \pi^2/2)/(\pi(1-\pi))$ with
  $\pi = \E[p]$. \emph{Given the prevalence, AUROC is linear in the outcome
  regression.} \Cref{thm:sharp-auc} turns this into an exact $O(n\log n)$
  algorithm for the sharp interval: a continuous knapsack inside, a
  ratio-of-quadratics line search outside. \Cref{prop:unified} extends it to a
  class of functionals that recovers every fixed-threshold metric as a special
  case. We also show the natural heuristic --- evaluate at the corners of the
  identified set --- is \emph{not valid}, because it ignores the coupling through
  the prevalence.

\item \textbf{A learning algorithm, not an audit} (\cref{sec:learning}).
  \Cref{thm:dcl} minimises the worst-case risk over the identified set. The
  inner supremum has a closed form: the risk at the midpoint model plus a margin
  penalty weighted by the local identification width. Contrary to what one might
  expect, \emph{no convex relaxation is required} --- the exact objective is a
  supremum of convex functions and hence convex, which is a strictly stronger
  statement than tractability via relaxation and distinguishes the problem from
  $f$-divergence and Wasserstein DRO, where the inner problem needs duality. A
  saddle point exists, the least-favourable member of the identified set is
  explicit, and the Bayes act is an \emph{interval-shrunk logit} with an
  abstention band. \Cref{prop:regret} gives the companion minimax-\emph{regret}
  rule in closed form as a difference quotient of the binary entropy.

\item \textbf{$\Gamma$ is unavoidable, but falsifiable}
  (\cref{sec:impossibility}). \Cref{thm:impossible} is a finite-sample two-point
  argument: for \emph{every} $n$, no estimator of deployment risk can be
  accurate uniformly over censoring mechanisms, and $\Gamma$ itself is not
  identified from $(X, T, TY)$ alone. \Cref{prop:falsify} is the constructive
  complement: when several decision makers with differing leniency handle
  comparable cases, $\dcsm(\Gamma)$ acquires testable implications and a
  \emph{lower} bound $\Gamma_{\min}$ is identified. In simulation the test
  recovers the true $\Gamma_0$ to three decimals.

\item \textbf{Generalisation, linear in $\log\Gamma$}
  (\cref{sec:generalization}). \Cref{thm:generalization} bounds the excess
  worst-case risk of the empirical minimiser; the complexity term grows like
  $\log\Gamma$, not like $\Gamma$, because $\Gamma$ acts as a \emph{translation}
  on the log-odds scale. The empirical scaling matches.

\item \textbf{Uncertainty quantification is biased, undetectably}
  (\cref{sec:uncertainty}). \Cref{cor:uq} shows the observed-data regression
  $p_1(x)$ always lies \emph{inside} the identified set, so the naive
  aleatoric/epistemic split can never be refuted by a calibration or
  goodness-of-fit check --- it is not wrong on the data, it answers a different
  question. Meanwhile the epistemic term vanishes with $n$ while the
  identification width does not, so any two-way decomposition is asymptotically
  overconfident by a non-vanishing amount.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Empirical study.} We evaluate on four testbeds
(\cref{sec:experiments}): a synthetic benchmark whose oracle quantities are
exact by quadrature, so sharpness can be verified numerically; real Lending Club
applications in which the hidden signal is the underwriter's own assigned
interest rate; a simulated MIMIC-IV emergency-department cohort standing in for
credentialed data we document how to obtain; and COMPAS, the rare selective
labels instance in which the censored labels \emph{were} recorded, so the bounds
can be checked against the truth on genuinely real decisions. There, coverage is
$0\%$ under missing-at-random, and $100\%$ exactly at the $\Gamma$ implied by the
data's own detained-versus-released odds ratio.
