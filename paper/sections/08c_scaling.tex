\paragraph{The rate.} \Cref{tab:scaling} and \cref{fig:scaling} report the two
panels. Averaged over $\Gamma$, the uniform deviation decays with
exponent $-0.507$ against the $-0.5$ the bound predicts, and every individual
cell lies in $[-0.534, -0.490]$. This is the term \cref{thm:generalization}
controls, and it behaves exactly as stated.

\paragraph{The $\Gamma$-dependence is logarithmic.} Regressing the fitted
$n^{-1/2}$ coefficient on $\log\Gamma$ gives $R^{2} = 0.89$; regressing it on
$\Gamma$ gives $R^{2} = 0.60$. Over a $16$-fold range of $\Gamma$ the coefficient
grows by only $24\%$ --- consistent with the $\log\Gamma$ of
\cref{cor:loggamma} and not with a linear dependence on $\Gamma$, which would
have predicted more than an order of magnitude. This is the payoff of
parameterising the sensitivity model on the log-odds scale, where $\Gamma$ acts
as a translation rather than a multiplicative blow-up (\cref{lem:box}).

\paragraph{A caveat we will not paper over.} The \emph{excess risk of the
empirical minimiser} --- what a practitioner would measure --- decays much faster
than $n^{-1/2}$, with exponent $-1.08$ across all $\Gamma$. That is the familiar
fast-rate phenomenon for a strongly convex, well-specified objective, and it is
\emph{consistent with} \cref{thm:generalization} (an upper bound) without being
evidence \emph{for} its rate. Reporting the excess risk as a confirmation of the
$n^{-1/2}$ rate would be a mistake, which is why we measure the uniform
deviation instead.

\Cref{tab:uq} and \cref{fig:uq} separate three things that are easy to conflate.

\emph{Identification (Panel A).} With oracle nuisances every claim of
\cref{cor:uq} holds exactly: $p_1(x)$ lies inside the identified set for
$100\%$ of units at every sample size, and both the true and the naive mean
aleatoric entropies fall inside the sharp identified interval. The naive
estimate overstates aleatoric uncertainty by $0.05$ nats --- about $10\%$ of the
true value --- and, being inside the identified set, it cannot be refuted by any
test on observed data. That is the corollary's uncomfortable half, confirmed.

\emph{Asymptotic overconfidence (Panel C).} For the converged ensemble the
reported epistemic term falls by $10.5\times$ between $n=2{,}000$ and
$n=30{,}000$ and by $23\times$ at $n=70{,}000$ (fitted exponent $-0.87$, i.e.\
roughly $n^{-1}$), while the censoring term moves by $13\%$ in total and with no
trend. At the largest sample the ratio is $0.005$: \emph{the uncertainty a modern
system reports is two orders of magnitude smaller than the identification
uncertainty it omits.}

\emph{A different pathology, and a detectable one.} The fixed-budget deep
ensemble's ``epistemic'' term does not contract at all (fitted exponent $-0.12$)
and exceeds the censoring term throughout --- but that is optimisation noise, not
posterior concentration, and its calibration error is $5$--$10\times$ larger.
Unlike the bias \cref{cor:uq} describes, this failure \emph{is} visible from
observed data: a calibration check catches it. We report it because the two are
easy to confuse, and only one of them is the paper's point.

\paragraph{Estimated nuisances are the binding constraint.} One caveat is
important enough to state separately. All of the above is \emph{identification}:
it assumes $e$ and $p_1$ are known. With cross-fitted gradient-boosted estimates
(mean absolute error $0.05$--$0.09$ on this benchmark) the identified set
contains the true $p(x)$ for only $45$--$58\%$ of units. Aggregate functionals
are far more forgiving --- the AUROC intervals of \cref{tab:sharpness} still cover
$100\%$ of the time, because pointwise errors average out --- but \emph{pointwise}
guarantees inherit the full nuisance error. Tightening that, most plausibly via
a Neyman-orthogonal version of the bound map, is the most valuable piece of
follow-up work we can identify.
