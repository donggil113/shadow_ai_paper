\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Testbeds.} \textbf{SL-Bench} is synthetic with exact oracle
quantities: covariates, a latent decision-maker signal $S$ entering both the
outcome and the decision, and several decision makers with randomly assigned
leniency. All population quantities --- $p$, $e$, $p_1$, $p_0$ and the realised
$\Gamma_0$ --- are computed by Gauss--Hermite quadrature, and the confounding
scale is bisected \emph{on the instance} so that the realised $\Gamma_0$ matches
its target. \textbf{Lending Club} uses real applicant features and real
repayment outcomes ($n=9{,}857$, $5.2\%$ adverse); the hidden signal $S$ is the
\emph{underwriter's own assigned interest rate and sub-grade}, a real variable
that genuinely predicts default and is genuinely absent from application data, so
the confounding is real rather than invented; the funding decision is drawn from
a $\Gamma_0$-calibrated policy in $(X,S)$. \textbf{MIMIC-sim} is a simulated
emergency-department cohort in which troponin ordering censors the ACS label.
\textbf{COMPAS} ($n=6{,}054$ after ProPublica's cohort filter) is the one testbed
with \emph{real decisions and real censored outcomes}: judges' pretrial release
decisions are real, and two-year rearrest is recorded for detained and released
defendants alike, so we hide the detained defendants' labels from every method
and still evaluate against the truth. \textbf{AER-CreditCard} has fully real
accept/reject decisions and an outcome that is structurally unobservable for
rejected applicants --- bounds are the only reportable object there.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{On the medical testbed.} MIMIC-IV is a credentialed PhysioNet resource
and \texttt{physionet.org} was unreachable from the environment in which this
work was produced. \texttt{dcl/data/mimic.py} implements the full cohort-building
pipeline against the real file layout and documents the download; the numbers
reported under ``MIMIC-sim'' come from the accompanying \emph{simulator} and
should be read as a demonstration of the pipeline, not as a clinical result.
\end{quote}

Every number is out of sample: methods are fit on a training split and evaluated
on a disjoint test split, with cross-fitted, isotonically calibrated nuisances
(\cref{app:nuisance}). Results are averaged over three seeds.

\Cref{tab:sharpness} and \cref{fig:sharpness} report three things. First,
\emph{sharpness}: an independent projected-gradient search over the identified
box never escapes the analytic interval and matches it to $3.6\times10^{-6}$,
while the extremal $p^\star$ returned by \cref{thm:sharp-auc} lies in the box and
attains the bound to machine precision. Second, \emph{validity}: whenever
$\Gamma\ge\Gamma_0$ the sharp interval covers the true deployment AUROC in
$100\%$ of cells, and degrades to $71\%$ --- gracefully, not catastrophically ---
when the assumed $\Gamma$ is too small. Corner evaluation covers in $10\%$ of
cells: it is $14\times$ narrower than the sharp interval and, as
\cref{prop:corner} predicts and \cref{app:counterexample} exhibits explicitly,
narrow for the wrong reason. Bounding numerator and denominator separately is
valid but $3.2\times$ wider, which is what sharpness buys. Third, \emph{cost}:
$400{,}000$ units in $3.4$ seconds.

\subsection{Real decisions: COMPAS}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/fig2_compas}
\caption{\textbf{Real pretrial release decisions with real censored outcomes.}
Intervals that contain the true deployment AUROC are drawn in colour, those that
miss it in grey. Assuming missing-at-random ($\Gamma=1$) collapses the interval
to a point that misses; coverage begins exactly at the $\Gamma$ implied by the
data's own detained-versus-released odds ratio, $1.885$.}
\label{fig:compas}
\end{figure}

COMPAS lets us check the bounds against ground truth on decisions no one
simulated (\cref{fig:compas}). The empirical odds ratio between the detained and
released groups' rearrest rates is $1.885$. At $\Gamma=1$ --- the
missing-at-random assumption that IPW and AIPW require --- the interval collapses
to a point and covers the truth in $0\%$ of splits. Coverage reaches $100\%$
exactly at $\Gamma = 1.885$, where the interval has width $0.099$: narrow enough
to be useful, wide enough to be honest. Corner evaluation covers in $0\%$ of
splits at every $\Gamma$ we tried. \Cref{tab:compas} repeats the method comparison on this testbed. The ordering is
the same as on the benchmarks: the minimax-regret rule certifies a regret
$11\times$ tighter than ERM and $15$--$19\times$ tighter than the parametric
selection model, while its realised risk ($0.625$) is lower than every baseline
except one Heckman fit, and lower than the \emph{oracle}'s ($0.631$). Heckman's
realised risk is also the least stable across splits ($0.616$--$0.633$), which is
what one expects of a method whose identification rests on a functional form.

We read all of this as the strongest available
evidence that $\dcsm(\Gamma)$ with a $\Gamma$ anchored to observable leniency or
odds-ratio structure is the right object to report --- while repeating the caveat
of \cref{sec:setup}: pretrial detention is partly incapacitating, so $1.885$
should be read as an anchor, not an estimate of $\Gamma_0$.

\Cref{tab:learning} and \cref{fig:learning} report the trade-off honestly.

\emph{The certificates.} DCL attains the best worst-case risk on every testbed,
by construction --- but the margin is what matters: $0.2299$ against ERM's
$0.3015$ on Lending Club, $0.2830$ against $0.3878$ on MIMIC-sim. Certified
regret separates the methods far more sharply still: the minimax-regret rule
\eqref{eq:regret-rule} cuts it by $35\times$ against ERM on Lending Club,
$13\times$ on MIMIC-sim and $4.5\times$ on SL-Bench. Notably the \emph{oracle} ---
fit on the uncensored labels --- has poor certified regret ($0.0428$, $0.0703$,
$0.1230$): it is tuned to one member of the identified set and is not robust to
the others. Being right about the truth and being robust are different
properties.

\emph{The realised metrics.} On two of three testbeds the minimax-regret rule
also has the \emph{lowest realised deployment risk of any method}, the oracle
included ($0.1967$ vs.\ $0.2213$ on Lending Club; $0.2431$ vs.\ $0.2520$ on
MIMIC-sim) --- not because it knows more, but because shrinking a calibrated
estimate toward the decision boundary is a better-conditioned estimator than
fitting flexibly to labels. On SL-Bench, where the latent signal is strong and
the Heckman model's normality assumption happens to hold by construction, the
parametric selection model attains the best realised risk and DCL pays a small
price for not assuming a functional form. The minimax-\emph{risk} rule is
uniformly more conservative: it buys the tightest certificate and gives up
realised accuracy, which is the robustness trade-off stated plainly. Manski
($\Gamma=\infty$) is valid and useless everywhere, with realised risk roughly
double ERM's.

\emph{The blind spot.} The observed AUROC --- the number a practitioner would put
in a paper --- differs from the true deployment AUROC by $-0.018$ on Lending Club
and $-0.017$ on MIMIC-sim. The gap is not always in the flattering direction, and
that is precisely the point: without a sensitivity analysis one cannot know its
sign.
